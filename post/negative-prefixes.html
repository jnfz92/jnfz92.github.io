Negative Prefixes in English

Learn how un-, in-, im-, dis- and mis- change meaning, with practical rules and bilingual examples.

Detailed guide · Recommended level B1–B2

In this article
  1. Meaning and use
  2. Bilingual examples
  3. Rules and common mistakes
  4. Practice

1. Meaning and use

A negative prefix changes a word toward an opposite, negative, or incorrect meaning. English uses several prefixes, so learning common patterns is more reliable than translating directly from Spanish.

Un- is very common: happy → unhappy; employed → unemployed. In- often appears with Latin roots: accurate → inaccurate; dependent → independent. Before m or p, in- commonly becomes im-: possible → impossible; patient → impatient.

2. Bilingual examples

After losing his contract, he found himself unemployed for several months.
Después de perder su contrato, estuvo desempleado durante varios meses.
The information was inaccurate.
La información era imprecisa.
I misunderstood the question.
Malinterpreté la pregunta.

3. Rules and common mistakes

Dis- often expresses an opposite or absence: honest → dishonest; agree → disagree. Mis- usually means wrongly or badly: understand → misunderstand; spell → misspell.

Do not invent a prefix by analogy. English says impossible, not “unpossible”. Learn the whole word when possible.

Study tip: Read the English sentence aloud first. Try to explain it before checking the Spanish translation.

4. Practice

  1. Make the opposite: possible, employed, accurate, honest. — Forma el contrario: posible, empleado, preciso, honesto.
  2. Choose the correct word: I ___ the instructions. (misunderstood / disunderstood) — Elige la palabra correcta: «Entendí mal las instrucciones».

Answers: impossible, unemployed, inaccurate, dishonest. · misunderstood
